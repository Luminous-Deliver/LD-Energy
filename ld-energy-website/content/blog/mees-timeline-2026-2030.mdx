The current legal minimum and the future policy are different. Domestic MEES requires **EPC E** for rentals within scope unless a valid exemption is registered. Government has confirmed a higher standard for **1 October 2030**, but its implementation still requires legislation.

## The timeline

| Date | Status and meaning |
|---|---|
| April 2018 | E minimum applied to new tenancies within scope |
| April 2020 | E minimum also applied to continuing tenancies within scope |
| January 2026 | Government response confirmed the higher standard and a single 2030 compliance date |
| 2027 | Government's intended implementation timetable for legislation, not proof that it is already law |
| Before 1 October 2029 | Confirmed transitional policy recognises existing EPC Energy Efficiency Ratings of C or above until expiry or replacement |
| 1 October 2030 | Confirmed compliance date for all tenancies in scope under the higher standard, subject to implementing legislation and exemptions |

There is no separate earlier new-tenancy phase in the confirmed policy. The new standard uses **fabric performance plus heating-system performance or smart readiness**, rather than simply applying today's cost-based rating to every home.

## What applies today

The current E minimum applies to specified domestic tenancies where an EPC is required. A covered F- or G-rated property cannot be let or continue to be let without a valid registered exemption. Financial penalties are capped at £5,000 per property in total under current guidance.

The E-standard spending cap remains £3,500 including VAT. The confirmed future policy uses £10,000 and a longer cost-cap exemption period, but those changes are not current requirements. The planned £30,000 penalty maximum also needs legislation.

## Plan from the individual property

### Establish the current position

Check the EPC register, tenancy type, validity and any exemption. A few years of age alone does not invalidate an EPC. A new assessment can be useful where the property has changed or the existing information needs checking, but it is not a guaranteed upgrade.

Read the standard software-generated recommendations, which are included with the EPC where applicable. Abdul's optional £35 Improvement Plan adds personalised interpretation and priorities with the full Elmhurst Energy Report; it is not needed to obtain the standard recommendations.

### Investigate suitable measures

Lighting, heating controls, cylinder insulation, loft insulation and draught-proofing may be relevant. Suitability and effect depend on what is already installed and how the whole dwelling is modelled. Do not add assumed point gains or infer a budget to reach C from the starting band alone.

For larger projects, consider wall insulation, heating changes, glazing or solar only with suitable installation advice. Permission, moisture risk, ventilation, access and the property's construction can all affect the decision. Preserve evidence of completed work for a future assessment.

See [how to improve an EPC rating](/blog/how-to-improve-epc-rating) for more on property-specific factors.

### Check funding before committing

GBIS ended on 31 March 2026. ECO4 has been extended to 31 December 2026 to complete existing obligations and remedial work, without an increased target. Eligibility does not guarantee that funding or an installer slot is available. Check with the supplier or local authority before assuming a project will be funded.

### Schedule and reassess where appropriate

Planning ahead gives time to obtain advice, permissions and quotes. It does not guarantee cheaper work or shorter lead times. Coordinate access with tenants, and check the final regulations before relying on a future compliance route.

Improvements do not automatically invalidate an existing EPC. A fresh assessment can record the changed property and establish a new rating; the result must come from the assessed data and current methodology.

## Exemptions are conditional

The all-improvements-made and high-cost exemptions are separate routes. Other routes include third-party consent, devaluation and certain wall-insulation risks. Each requires its own evidence and registration. Listed status alone is not a MEES exemption, and not every new landlord has a six-month exemption.

Most current exemptions last five years, with important exceptions such as a tenancy ending after tenant refusal. Check the specific expiry and ownership rules. An improvement plan is not itself an exemption.
