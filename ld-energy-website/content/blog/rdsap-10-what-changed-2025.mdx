RdSAP 10 is the updated Reduced data Standard Assessment Procedure methodology that replaced RdSAP 9.94 on 15 June 2025. It is used for existing-dwelling assessments; new-build assessments use full SAP. Changes include more detailed window, ventilation and lighting data, expanded building-element options and recognition of newer technologies.

The methodology has not stood still since. The conventions assessors work to were revised again on **6 August 2026**, in version 12.2. That update is covered further down.

## What is RdSAP and why does it matter?

SAP (Standard Assessment Procedure) is the UK government-approved method for calculating the energy performance of dwellings. The full SAP is used for new buildings; the **Reduced data SAP (RdSAP)** is used for existing homes, "reduced" because some inputs are estimated from construction type and age rather than directly measured.

RdSAP determines your EPC rating. The methodology sets the rules for what data the assessor collects, what defaults apply when data is unavailable, and how inputs are weighted in the final score. Changes to RdSAP can change ratings even if nothing about the property has changed.

RdSAP 9.94 had been in use for over a decade without a major update. RdSAP 10 is a significant revision.

## The main changes in RdSAP 10

### 1. Energy costs and carbon are different measures

The headline Energy Efficiency Rating is based on modelled energy costs. Carbon and primary-energy figures describe different aspects of performance. A change in the electricity carbon factor does not, by itself, establish whether a property's headline band goes up or down.

### 2. More detailed building information

RdSAP 10 collects window measurements and more detail about ventilation and room-in-roof construction. It also provides expanded options for insulation and building elements. Acceptable supporting evidence can affect which inputs the assessor records.

### 3. Heating and newer technologies

The calculation considers the heating system and applicable product data. RdSAP 10 also recognises technologies including PV battery storage and diverters. No heating technology guarantees a particular band or a fixed improvement over an older assessment.

See [Elmhurst's RdSAP 10 overview](https://www.elmhurstenergy.co.uk/blog/2025/05/13/rdsap-10-recap/) for the methodology changes. The property's recorded data and approved calculation determine its result.

### 4. A more detailed lighting count

Lighting is counted lamp by lamp, not fitting by fitting. The rules assessors follow are specific:

- **LED lamps are recorded as LED.** Only LEDs.
- **Fluorescent tubes and compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) are recorded as CFL.** They are counted separately from LEDs, not merged with them.
- **Tungsten and halogen lamps are recorded as incandescent.** These are not low energy, and that includes low-voltage halogen downlights, which people often assume are efficient because they are small.
- **LED strip lighting counts as one lamp per running metre**, rounded to the nearest metre.
- Every lamp in a **permanent fitting** counts, including fixed under-cupboard kitchen lights. Table lamps, standard lamps and anything else plugged into a socket do not.
- If a lamp is **missing or its type cannot be identified**, that individual lamp is recorded as CFL.
- If **more than half** the non-incandescent lamps are missing or cannot be identified, a different rule takes over and they are all recorded as low-energy lighting rather than counted one by one.

**Effect:** the lighting section records what is actually installed and identifiable on the day of the visit, and it distinguishes LED from CFL rather than treating them as one category. The assessment reflects the property as found — that is the rule, not a loophole. So if halogen or incandescent lamps have already been replaced with LEDs, that work counts; lamps sitting in a box out of their fittings do not, because a missing lamp is recorded as CFL. If most of the non-incandescent lamps are missing, the more-than-half rule above applies instead. LED lamps are recorded as LED; halogen and incandescent are recorded as not low-energy, whatever their wattage.

### 5. Changes to conservatory treatment

RdSAP 10 updates how conservatories and extensions are treated. Unheated conservatories attached to the main building now have revised thermal buffering values. Heated conservatories are included in the heated floor area calculation with updated default U-values for their glazed construction.

**Effect:** Properties with conservatories may see minor changes in SAP score. The direction depends on construction quality and whether the conservatory is heated.

### 6. Updated primary energy factors

Primary energy factors (which convert delivered energy to the energy consumed in extraction, processing, and distribution) have been revised for all fuel types. These affect the EPC's primary energy indicator rather than the SAP score directly.

## What changed again in August 2026

RdSAP 10 is the methodology. Sitting on top of it is a separate document, the **RdSAP conventions**, which tells assessors how to handle situations the methodology alone does not settle. It is updated more often than the methodology, and where the two disagree, the convention wins.

The current edition is **version 12.2, issued 13 July 2026 and in force from 6 August 2026**. Four conventions in it are genuinely new rather than reworded. The two most likely to affect a homeowner are the conservatory definition and heat-as-a-service.

### Conservatories now have a hard definition

For the first time the conventions state exactly what counts as a conservatory: **at least 50% of the external heat-loss walls glazed, and at least 75% of the roof glazed**.

That matters because of what follows. If a conservatory no longer meets the roof test, because the glazed roof has been **replaced with a solid one** or a **false ceiling has been fitted**, it is no longer assessed as a conservatory at all. It becomes an **extension**, and it is brought inside the heated envelope of the property.

**Why this catches people out:** replacing a conservatory roof is a common improvement, usually sold on comfort rather than efficiency. It changes how the room is assessed. Whether that helps or hurts the rating depends on the property and on what went in above the ceiling — there is no single answer. With visual or documentary evidence of insulation, the assessor records the insulation that is there. Without evidence, and if the roof is pitched above 10 degrees with no access to the void, the convention requires it to be recorded as having no insulation.

**What to do:** if you have had a conservatory roof replaced, find the paperwork before the assessment — an installer's specification, a building control notice, or a guarantee that names the insulation. It lets the assessor record what is genuinely in place rather than fall back on the no-insulation default. It is evidence of the real construction, not a way to change the outcome.

### Heat as a service is treated as community heating

Where a provider **owns or operates the heat generator** and sells you heat as a service, it is now assessed as community heating, whether or not the equipment sits inside your property. Electric systems are treated as a community heat pump, everything else as a community boiler. Documentary evidence is required to confirm the arrangement, so keep the contract.

### Electric combi boilers have their own instruction

Electric combi boilers are now recorded in a defined way rather than left to interpretation. Water heating from one is treated as instantaneous at point of use.

### Two ventilation systems in one home

Where a property has two mechanical ventilation systems, the conventions now set out which one is entered, in a defined order of precedence, rather than leaving it to the assessor.

### Smaller changes worth knowing

- **Solar batteries:** a single battery larger than 5 kW is now entered as two — a first entry of 5 kW and the remainder as a second battery. If you have battery storage, have the manufacturer, model and usable capacity to hand, otherwise a default figure is used.
- **Air pressure tests:** if material changes have been made to the property since the original test, the certificate is no longer valid for the assessment.
- **Secondary glazing:** unusual arrangements, such as plastic glazing units fitted over single glass, are now explicitly treated as secondary glazing.

## What hasn't changed

Reassurance matters here, because methodology changes cause anxiety among landlords with recently issued EPCs. The following remain identical under RdSAP 10:

- The **A–G band structure**, SAP 69+ is still C, SAP 55–68 is still D, SAP 39–54 is still E. The thresholds have not moved.
- The **core data collection categories**, assessors still visit and record wall type, roof, glazing, heating, hot water and lighting. The categories are the same, though the level of detail recorded within some of them has increased, and the conventions updates keep adding to that detail rather than replacing it.
- The **10-year validity period**, EPCs issued under RdSAP 9.94 remain valid for their full 10-year term. The methodology change is not a reason to renew.
- The **MEES framework**, the current E minimum still applies to rentals within scope unless exempt. The confirmed higher standard for 1 October 2030 requires implementing legislation; it is not current law.
- The **government EPC register**, certificates are still lodged at the same register; the interface and search function are unchanged.
- The **accreditation schemes**, Elmhurst, ECMK, Quidos, and other approved schemes continue to operate as before.

## Do you need to re-assess your property?

**No, if:** Your existing EPC was issued under RdSAP 9.94 and is within its 10-year validity. The old certificate remains legally valid for selling or letting.

**Yes, if:**
- Your certificate has expired (over 10 years old)
- You've made improvements since the last assessment and want the updated rating
- You want to ensure your rating reflects the latest methodology (relevant if your SAP score is close to a band boundary)

A score close to a band boundary does not establish whether reassessment will move it up or down. Consider the reason for an updated certificate and the property evidence before commissioning another assessment.

## Practical implications for London landlords and sellers

The current MEES minimum remains E for rentals within scope unless exempt. The confirmed 2030 policy uses reformed metrics, so it must not be described simply as applying today's SAP 69 threshold to every tenancy.

For a portfolio, check current compliance and the future transitional rules separately. A new assessment establishes a result; it does not guarantee C.

For sellers, the practical impact is similar. If your agent or solicitor is querying a borderline rating, a fresh assessment under RdSAP 10 may resolve the question with better data.
