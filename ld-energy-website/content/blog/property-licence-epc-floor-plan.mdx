Many London councils license privately rented homes, and almost every licence application asks something about the property's EPC or its layout. The papers differ from one council to the next, even for the same type of licence. This guide covers what councils ask for, why, and where to find your own council's list.

## The three kinds of licence

- **Mandatory HMO licence:** needed everywhere in England for a home rented to five or more people who form more than one household and share a toilet, bathroom or kitchen.
- **Additional HMO licence:** a council can extend HMO licensing to smaller shared homes, usually three or four people from two or more households. Many London boroughs do this across the whole borough.
- **Selective licence:** covers ordinary rented homes let to one household or two sharers, in the wards a council has designated.

A licence lasts up to five years, and the council sets the fee. Which of the three you need depends on who lives in the home and where it is.

## Where the EPC comes in

Councils use the EPC in three different ways, and some use more than one:

- **As a required document.** Newham's online application will not let you continue without the EPC, a gas safety certificate and an EICR. Brent asks for the current EPC rated A to E unless a valid exemption is in place. Redbridge says it only approves licences for properties rated A to E.
- **As a condition of a fee discount.** Wandsworth, Waltham Forest, Lambeth, Islington, Haringey, Westminster, Hackney, Havering, Enfield and Newham all link a lower fee to the rating, usually for a C or better. Some, such as Havering and Enfield, withdraw the discount if the documents arrive late or the council has had to chase.
- **As a factor in how long the licence lasts.** Haringey says an application without a valid EPC gets a licence of one year instead of five, and lists a failed EPC as a reason to shorten it by up to four years.

Waltham Forest adds that it may refuse the discount if it thinks the EPC is not a true reflection of the home. An accurate, recent certificate avoids that question.

Other councils, including Merton, Harrow, Bromley and Kingston, list no fee linked to the EPC. You still need one to let the home: a rented home in England needs a valid EPC rated E or above unless an exemption is registered. Our [guide for landlords](/blog/epc-for-landlords-2026) covers that rule.

## What the floor plan has to show

Where a council asks for a floor plan, it is to check that the rooms suit the number of people living there and that the layout is safe. What they ask the plan to show varies:

- **Room sizes.** Brent asks for the size of every room in square metres for HMO licences. Tower Hamlets asks for floor plans with room sizes. Sutton and Kingston ask for a current floor plan and accurate measurements for all rooms.
- **Every part of the layout.** Croydon asks for every floor level, room, window, door and staircase, with what each room is used for and its size in square metres.
- **Fire safety.** Redbridge asks for fire exits and the positions of smoke alarms and heat detectors. Tower Hamlets asks you to know where the fire detection and sounders are.
- **Annotations.** Lambeth asks for an annotated floor plan.

Redbridge says the plan does not need to be drawn by an architect or surveyor, and Bexley asks only for a simple plan. Hounslow charges extra for help measuring the rooms and drawing the sketch plan.

## Why room sizes matter for an HMO

HMO licences in England include minimum sizes for rooms used as bedrooms:

- **One person over 10:** at least 6.51 square metres.
- **Two people over 10:** at least 10.22 square metres.
- **One child under 10:** at least 4.64 square metres.
- **Under 4.64 square metres:** the room cannot be used as a bedroom at all.

Any part of a room where the ceiling is lower than 1.5 metres does not count towards its floor area. That matters in loft rooms and under sloping ceilings, where a room can look big enough and still fall short.

The EPC does not answer this. It records the total floor area of the home, not the size of each room, so councils that check room sizes need the floor plan.
