Every EPC gives a property a score from 1 to 100 under the Standard Assessment Procedure, then maps that score to a letter band from A to G. The letter on the certificate is just the band your score landed in.

The score is **modelled**, not metered. It comes from the building itself — construction, insulation, glazing, heating, hot water and lighting — so it does not change because you are careful with the thermostat.

## The EPC band scale at a glance

| Band | SAP score | In short |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **A** | 92–100 | Exceptional. Rare outside purpose-built low-energy homes. |
| **B** | 81–91 | Very efficient. Common in recent new-build. |
| **C** | 69–80 | Good. The band most improvement work aims at. |
| **D** | 55–68 | Middle of the scale. The most looked-up band. |
| **E** | 39–54 | Below average, but still above the letting minimum. |
| **F** | 21–38 | Poor. Cannot normally be let without an exemption. |
| **G** | 1–20 | Lowest band. Cannot normally be let without an exemption. |

Those SAP ranges are fixed by the methodology, so an EPC grade of D always means 55–68 points, whatever the property.

## EPC rating A

Band A covers 92 to 100 SAP points and is the top of the scale. It is genuinely uncommon in existing housing.

Properties reaching band A are typically designed for it from the outset — a very well-insulated envelope, low-carbon heating, and usually on-site generation such as solar PV. Reaching A by retrofitting an older home is possible but rarely straightforward.

If you are buying and see an A, it is worth reading the certificate's detail rather than the letter alone: the measures behind it tell you what you are actually inheriting.

## EPC rating B

Band B is 81 to 91 points, and is where a lot of modern construction sits.

Homes in this band usually combine good fabric insulation with an efficient heating system, and often some renewable contribution. For most owners, B is the realistic ceiling without significant structural work.

## EPC rating C

Band C runs from 69 to 80 points. It is the band most improvement work targets, and the one most often mentioned in policy discussion.

A property in band C generally has the fundamentals right — reasonable insulation, functional modern heating, efficient lighting — without necessarily having anything exceptional. It is comfortably above the current minimum for letting.

## EPC rating D

Band D covers 55 to 68 SAP points and sits squarely in the middle of the A–G scale. It is also, by some distance, the band people most often look up — which is worth a little more detail.

**What band D usually signals.** A D generally means the basics are in place but something meaningful is holding the score back. Properties in this band may have:

- solid walls, or cavity walls that have not been filled
- partial loft insulation, or insulation that could not be inspected on the day
- older glazing, or a mix of glazing ages across the property
- a functional but dated boiler, or limited heating controls
- an uninsulated or under-insulated hot-water cylinder
- the lamp counts and types recorded in permanent fittings

None of those is universal. Two properties can both land in band D for completely different reasons, and the certificate's own detail is what tells you which reason applies to yours.

**What it means practically.** A D is above the current MEES minimum of band E, so a rented property in band D is not, on that basis alone, prevented from being let. For sale, a D is unremarkable — it is neither a selling point nor an obstacle in most of the market.

**One caution on comparisons.** Band D spans fourteen SAP points, so two homes can both be "a D" and be some distance apart. A 56 and a 68 are very different properties. If you are comparing certificates, compare the scores and the recorded details, not just the letters. ## EPC rating E

Band E is 39 to 54 points — below average, but still at or above the legal minimum for letting a domestic property under MEES.

Properties here often have more than one limiting factor at once: an uninsulated wall type combined with older heating, for example. Because E is the current threshold for rented property, landlords in this band have the least margin. Anything that later reduces the recorded score could take a property below the minimum at its next assessment.

## EPC rating F

Band F covers 21 to 38 points. Under the MEES Regulations, a property in band F cannot normally be let unless a valid exemption is registered.

An F generally reflects several significant issues together rather than one weak component — commonly an uninsulated envelope alongside inefficient or non-central heating.

## EPC rating G

Band G is the lowest, at 1 to 20 points. As with F, a property in band G cannot normally be let without a registered exemption.

Band G is usually a combination of an uninsulated building fabric, no effective central heating, and single glazing. It is worth checking the recorded details closely: where a home has genuinely been improved but the work could not be inspected or evidenced, the methodology may have had to apply a default assumption instead.

## How the band is actually produced

Two things are worth being clear about, because they cause most of the confusion.

**The assessor does not choose the band.** An accredited Domestic Energy Assessor surveys the property, records its measurements, construction, insulation, glazing, heating, hot water and lighting, and verifies any evidence they can accept. That data goes into government-approved software, which produces the SAP score and the band. There is no field to adjust.

**Evidence changes what can be recorded.** Where an improvement cannot be inspected — insulation behind plasterboard, under a fitted floor, or above a boarded loft — the methodology may require a default assumption unless there is documentation confirming what was installed. That default can be less favourable than reality. Our [preparation and evidence guide](/preparing-for-your-epc#documents-and-evidence) sets out what is worth having to hand.

## What your band means if you rent the property out

Keeping this short, because it is a subject in its own right.

Under the Minimum Energy Efficiency Standard, since 1 April 2020 landlords cannot let or continue to let a property covered by the regulations if it has an EPC rating below band E, unless a valid exemption is registered. [GOV.UK's landlord guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/domestic-private-rented-property-minimum-energy-efficiency-standard-landlord-guidance) sets out the exemptions and how to register them.

Government has confirmed a higher standard equivalent to EPC C for 1 October 2030, using reformed EPC metrics. Implementing legislation is still required. The current E minimum remains in force for rentals within MEES scope, unless a valid exemption is registered.

Every lodged EPC is public. You can look up an existing certificate free on the [GOV.UK find an energy certificate service](https://www.gov.uk/find-energy-certificate) using the property's postcode.
