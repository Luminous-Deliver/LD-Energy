The five cheapest improvements — LED lighting, draught-proofing, a hot water cylinder jacket, heating controls and loft insulation — are inexpensive relative to fabric or heating work, potentially useful where suitable. Their effect depends on what is already installed and the assessment methodology; they do not guarantee a move to another band.

<EpcEffectNote />

## Why cheap improvements matter

Most of the marketing attention around EPC improvements focuses on heat pumps, external wall insulation, and solar panels, larger projects that cost thousands of pounds rather than hundreds. They matter for reaching band B or A. For a home moving from E to D, or D to C, the cheap measures are where to start.

Review low-cost measures where suitable, using the actual assessment. Their combined effect cannot be inferred from a budget or a fixed list of point gains.

## The five best improvements under £500

### 1. LED lighting throughout

RdSAP 10 records lamp counts and types in permanent fittings, distinguishing LED, fluorescent/CFL and incandescent lamps. A fitting with several lamps contributes several lamps to the count.

The effect of replacing older lamps depends on what is already installed and the whole-property calculation. Plug-in table lamps are not part of this count. See the [current lighting conventions](/blog/rdsap-10-what-changed-2025) for the assessment rules.

### 2. Loft insulation to 270mm

If you have an accessible pitched loft with less than 270mm of insulation, topping it up is the highest-impact of the cheap measures — and may qualify for funding, subject to current availability.

The assessment credits insulation depth, measured at the time of the visit. Ensure the loft is accessible for the assessor and the insulation is freshly laid and at depth.

**Funding:** GBIS ended on 31 March 2026. ECO4 ends on 31 December 2026, and funded work has to be finished by then. Check eligibility and actual funding availability with a supplier or local authority before committing to work.

### 3. Draught-proofing

Draught-proofing can reduce uncontrolled air leakage; an EPC visit is not an air-tightness test. Gaps around windows, doors, skirting boards, loft hatches, and pipework all allow cold air in and warm air out.

RdSAP 10 includes conventions for recording window and door draught-proofing. This does not give every draught-proofing measure a fixed point value. Preserve intentional ventilation and seek advice before blocking any opening.

**What to tackle:**
- Loft hatch: fit an insulated board cover
- External doors: fit brush or rubber seal strips
- Sash windows: fit brush strip around the frame
- Chimney (if unused): fit a chimney balloon or draught excluder
- Letterbox: fit an insulating cover

Draught-proofing is largely DIY-able, which is what keeps it cheap; an installer costs more but covers the whole property properly.

### 4. Hot water cylinder jacket

If your property has a separate hot water storage cylinder (rather than a combi boiler which heats water on demand), a well-insulated jacket dramatically reduces standing heat loss.

An uninsulated cylinder loses useful heat continuously to the surrounding air. RdSAP records cylinder insulation type and thickness separately, so an adequately insulated cylinder is credited where a bare one is not.

**Check:**
- Does your cylinder have a jacket? (A white or silver foil-wrapped jacket around the outside)
- Is it 80mm thick? (Check by feel, a thin jacket is 25mm, a proper one is 80mm+)

If it is uninsulated or thinly insulated, a jacket is inexpensive, widely available and takes about half an hour to fit. Pound for pound it is the best-value item on this list.

### 5. Heating controls

RdSAP 10 separately credits three control types:
- **Room thermostat:** the main heating is controlled by a thermostat in the living area, not just a timer.
- **Programmer (timer):** heating runs on a timed programme rather than manual-on-permanent.
- **Thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs):** each radiator can be controlled individually.

Each is recorded separately, so a property missing all three has more to gain than one that already has a thermostat.

Ask a heating engineer what controls are suitable for the existing system; the EPC effect depends on the system and approved calculation.

A smart-control brand alone does not establish its EPC treatment. The assessor records the identifiable control functions and applicable product information under the methodology.

**Installation:**
- Smart thermostat + programmer: DIY, or fitted by a heating engineer
- TRVs (replacing manual valves): inexpensive per radiator as a DIY job, or a modest plumber cost for the whole house

## Combining the five measures

These are options to investigate, not a universal order of works. The sensible sequence depends on suitability, existing systems and installation quotes.

What that sequence is actually worth on your property is calculated from the whole dwelling, and your EPC recommendation report shows an indicative rating after the recommended measures for that specific home.

The starting band or score cannot show whether lighting and loft insulation will be enough to reach C. Use the actual assessment and suitable installation advice.

Funding can change the budget, but confirm eligibility, availability and installation quotes before relying on it.

## What won't make the list cheap enough

These are commonly suggested but won't fit under £500 in most cases:
- **New boiler:** one of the biggest levers where the existing unit is an older non-condensing type, but well above a £500 budget.
- **Cavity wall insulation:** can cost more than £500 depending on wall area and access, and may be eligible for funding while schemes are open.
- **Solar PV:** costs thousands of pounds. Suitability and EPC effect depend on the property.
- **Double glazing:** expensive, and changes little where the property already has double glazing.
